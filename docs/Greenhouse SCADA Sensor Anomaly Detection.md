# **Advanced SCADA Integration and Sensor Anomaly Detection in Priva-Automated Greenhouse Environments**

The modern agricultural greenhouse represents a highly complex cyber-physical system characterized by intricate thermodynamic interactions, biological growth dynamics, and continuous environmental regulation. Central to this operation are Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) systems and climate process computers, which orchestrate heating, ventilation, air conditioning (HVAC), irrigation, and lighting based on real-time sensor telemetry. However, the harsh physical environment of a greenhouse—characterized by high humidity, extreme temperature fluctuations, chemical exposure, and dust—creates severe operational challenges for electronic sensors. When sensors degrade, drift, or fail, the resulting anomalous data can trigger cascading failures in automated control strategies, leading to suboptimal crop yields, resource waste, and catastrophic crop loss.  
Developing an advanced SCADA anomaly detection system requires a rigorous understanding of sensor fault etiology, the implementation of autonomous mathematical and machine learning frameworks, and precise integration pathways to extract both localized and cloud-based data from the underlying infrastructure. This analysis delineates the architectural and algorithmic requirements for building such a system, with a specific focus on interfacing with the Priva greenhouse climate control ecosystem, a leading standard in global horticulture automation.

## **Taxonomy and Etiology of Greenhouse Sensor Anomalies**

To engineer an effective anomaly detection module within a SCADA environment, the physical and statistical nature of sensor failures must be thoroughly classified. In precision agriculture, the manifestations of sensor degradation are fundamentally linked to the physical stressors of the greenhouse environment, and these failures dictate the mathematical models required to detect them. Sensor faults within Internet of Things (IoT) and SCADA networks are broadly classified into two primary domains: abrupt failures and incipient failures1.  
Abrupt failures, often termed hard faults, represent a complete cessation of sensor function, where no telemetry is collected or transmitted to the climate computer1. The root causes of hard faults are typically electrical or physical severances, including damaged sensor boards, disconnected communication cables, discharged power supplies, or catastrophic physical destruction1. From a SCADA architecture perspective, hard faults are generally trivial to detect. The sudden absence of a data stream, a complete loss of signal, or the triggering of a standard communication timeout protocol immediately flags the node as offline, prompting physical maintenance.  
Conversely, incipient failures, or soft faults, are significantly more insidious and represent the primary challenge for anomaly detection algorithms. In these scenarios, the sensor remains online and continues to transmit data packets, but the physical measurements it reports deviate from the true environmental state1. Because the data stream remains active and formatted correctly, basic connectivity monitoring systems fail to recognize the error. Consequently, the automated climate computer acts upon false information, potentially executing destructive climate control strategies1.

| Fault Classification | Phenomenological Definition | Primary Detection Challenge |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Calibration Drift** | A systematic, gradual divergence of the sensor's output from the true physical value over an extended period. | The slow rate of change avoids triggering static rate-of-change alarms or basic high/low thresholds until the deviation is severe1. |
| **Bias (Offset)** | A constant mathematical offset added to the true measurement value. The sensor accurately tracks environmental trends but reports values that are artificially shifted. | The sensor data still exhibits normal variance and diurnal patterns, masking the underlying inaccuracy from basic statistical checks1. |
| **Stuck Fault** | The sensor output freezes and reports a permanently constant value, regardless of physical changes in the environment. | Often occurs after a period of severe drift or hardware corrosion, requiring algorithms that monitor for unnatural lack of variance1. |
| **Accuracy Decline** | An overall reduction in the precision and reliability of the sensor readings, manifesting as increased signal noise or variance without a specific directional bias. | Difficult to distinguish from naturally turbulent environmental conditions, such as rapid wind speed changes affecting temperature readings1. |
| **Spike Faults** | Intermittent, sudden, and abnormally large deviations between consecutive measurements that return to baseline quickly. | Can be confused with legitimate, sudden mechanical state changes, such as the opening of a thermal screen or activation of a misting valve1. |

The manifestation of these soft faults is directly tied to the biological and environmental realities of greenhouse cultivation. Environmental stressors significantly influence sensor performance and dictate the specific physical degradation mechanisms that a SCADA system must monitor4. High humidity and persistent condensation are the primary vectors for sensor degradation. For instance, resistive soil moisture sensors, which expose their electrodes directly to the growing medium to measure electrical conductivity, are highly susceptible to rapid electrolytic corrosion1. In severe cases within precision agriculture testbeds, this corrosion can occur within a single week of deployment, typically resulting in a stuck fault where the sensor reports a constant zero percent soil moisture1.  
Capacitive sensors, which measure the dielectric constant of the soil through a protective barrier, are generally more resilient but remain vulnerable over extended periods. Water intrusion through pressed or glued protective layers can cause the sensor housing to bloat and short-circuit, resulting in either a constant zero percent reading or a stuck fault at an artificially high value1. Even advanced sensors coated in durable epoxy resins can develop micro-blisters due to continuous moisture exposure, which alters the dielectric field and leads to low-variance, persistently high moisture readings1. Furthermore, extreme heat accelerates sensor drift and probe wear, while saline irrigation water can skew electrical conductivity measurements, complicating data interpretation5.  
Airborne agricultural particulates present another significant stressor. Dust, pollen, and chemical residues from foliar sprays settle on sensor elements, physically obstructing them. In photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) sensors, dust accumulation directly reduces optical sensitivity, creating an artificial data trend that mimics a slow decline in solar radiation4. In gas sensors, such as those measuring carbon dioxide concentrations, particulate buildup on permeable membranes can skew readings and significantly slow the sensor's reaction time to environmental changes, causing lag in the SCADA control loops4. Additionally, extreme thermal cycling induces physical expansion and contraction within sensor electronics and housing materials. Over time, this thermal stress alters the internal electrical resistance of the components, contributing significantly to calibration drift and long-term accuracy decline4.

## **Statistical Distribution and Phenomenological Behavior of Greenhouse Data**

To accurately identify anomalies, a SCADA system must first establish a robust mathematical understanding of normal greenhouse operations. The data generated by a climate control system is highly dimensional, non-stationary, and heavily influenced by natural diurnal cycles and automated actuator interventions. Analyzing the distribution characteristics and correlation structures of this telemetry is a prerequisite for training effective machine learning models.  
Empirical studies on high-spatiotemporal-resolution greenhouse data reveal distinct statistical signatures for different environmental variables. The variance in these distributions dictates the type of algorithmic processing required.

| Sensor Variable | Statistical Distribution Characteristic | Operational Context |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Light Intensity** | Bimodal Distribution | Exhibits massive peaks during daytime high-intensity periods (e.g., 25,000–35,000 lux) and collapses to near-zero values during nighttime periods7. |
| **Temperature** | Near-Normal (Gaussian) Distribution | Tightly clustered around the climate computer's setpoint (e.g., centered at 24.5°C with a standard deviation of 3.2°C), reflecting the active intervention of the HVAC system7. |
| **Relative Humidity** | Right-Skewed Distribution | Values generally concentrate between 65% and 85% RH, with occasional, sharp drops below 50% RH occurring during active ventilation or dehumidification events7. |
| **Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)** | Multimodal Pattern | Displays baseline atmospheric levels with periodic, artificial elevations (e.g., up to 800 ppm) driven by automated CO₂ dosing, followed by sharp declines due to plant respiration and photosynthesis7. |

The temporal dynamics of these variables are heavily interdependent. Light intensity dictates the primary diurnal cycle, rising sharply after dawn and peaking around solar noon. Temperature exhibits a correlated but delayed pattern, typically demonstrating a two to three-hour lag behind light intensity due to the thermal mass of the greenhouse structure and the air volume7. Relative humidity displays an inverse relationship with temperature; as the air warms, its capacity to hold water vapor increases, causing relative humidity to drop unless active transpiration or misting compensates for the change7. Consequently, humidity reaches maximum values during the cooler early morning hours and minimum values during peak mid-afternoon temperatures7.  
From an anomaly detection perspective, the correlation structures between these variables provide critical validation pathways. The moderate negative correlation between temperature and humidity mirrors real-world thermodynamic dynamics8. However, comprehensive sensor arrays often exhibit weak overall correlations across disparate variables (e.g., light intensity versus soil moisture). This statistical independence implies that incipient anomalies frequently appear independently within a single sensor's data stream, rather than manifesting as simultaneous deviations across multiple features8. This independence challenges simple multi-sensor clustering frameworks, necessitating highly localized, sensor-specific anomaly evaluation combined with broader physical validation rules.

## **Algorithmic Frameworks for Autonomous Anomaly Detection**

Given the continuous, high-volume nature of greenhouse SCADA telemetry, manual identification of soft faults is impossible. The implementation of automated, algorithmic anomaly detection is a critical requirement for maintaining system integrity. The methodological landscape spans from enhanced statistical process control to advanced, physics-informed deep learning architectures.

### **Classical and Statistical Process Control**

Traditional SCADA systems rely heavily on static upper and lower alarm thresholds. While computationally trivial to implement, static thresholds fail comprehensively in the highly dynamic environment of a greenhouse. Because natural diurnal cycles cause massive, legitimate swings in temperature, humidity, and light, static limits must be set so wide to avoid false alarms that they become useless for detecting subtle calibration drift or accuracy decline7.  
Consequently, modern statistical methods evaluate the distribution and variance of the data stream. Methods such as Z-score and Interquartile Range (IQR) analysis characterization provide a baseline. The Z-score measures the number of standard deviations a specific data point resides from the historical mean, while the IQR assesses the spread of the middle fifty percent of the data to establish dynamic boundaries8. While these methods are effective for identifying obvious outliers and spike faults, they struggle with skewed distributions (such as humidity) and slow calibration drift, often over-flagging normal environmental variability as anomalous8.  
To address the limitations of static means, the Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) control chart is utilized. EWMA determines the statistical properties of a sensor's residual error under normal operation by applying heavier mathematical weighting to recent data points while exponentially discounting older observations. This establishes a dynamic, rolling threshold that adapts to minor, expected shifts in the environment, making it significantly more robust for identifying the precise moment a sensor begins an incipient drift trajectory10.

### **Deep Learning and Neural Architectures**

The proliferation of high-frequency IoT data has catalyzed the adoption of machine learning to map the complex, non-linear relationships inherent in greenhouse microclimates. These models learn the underlying patterns of the facility, enabling them to flag data that violates the learned norm.  
The Autoencoder family (including standard Autoencoders, Denoising Autoencoders, and Stacked Autoencoders) represents a powerful unsupervised learning approach11. An Autoencoder is a neural network designed to compress high-dimensional input data into a lower-dimensional latent space via an encoder, and subsequently reconstruct the original data from this latent representation via a decoder12. In the context of greenhouse anomaly detection, the network is trained exclusively on historical datasets containing normal, healthy sensor telemetry. This "zero-positive" learning paradigm is highly advantageous because collecting and labeling a comprehensive dataset of every possible sensor failure mode is practically impossible13. When anomalous data—such as a drifting humidity reading or a subtle bias offset—is fed into the trained Autoencoder during real-time SCADA inference, the model struggles to reconstruct the unfamiliar mathematical pattern. The resulting reconstruction error, defined as the mathematical difference between the input sequence and the output sequence, serves as the anomaly score. If this reconstruction error exceeds a dynamically calculated threshold, the data point is flagged as a fault8.  
For data possessing strong spatial and temporal dependencies, the CNN-LSTM-POT algorithm represents a state-of-the-art hybrid approach. Greenhouse telemetry contains both spatial dependencies (relationships between different sensors in the same physical compartment) and temporal dependencies (diurnal trends over time). This framework processes this dual-dimensional data through a highly structured pipeline7.  
Initially, a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is employed to extract local spatial features from the multi-source sensor array, mapping the instantaneous relationships between variables like CO₂, temperature, and light7. To ensure network stability and rapid convergence, the raw sensor inputs are subject to continuous min-max normalization within an active sliding window, bounding all highly disparate values (e.g., 35,000 lux versus 24°C) to a uniform scale between zero and one7. The extracted spatial feature maps are subsequently passed into a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network. The LSTM models the long-term temporal dependencies of the data, effectively learning the natural diurnal rhythms and thermal lag times of the specific greenhouse7. Based on this learned history, the LSTM outputs a precise prediction for the next expected sensor value.  
The critical final stage of this architecture is the adaptive thresholding module. The residual difference between the LSTM's prediction and the actual observed sensor value from the SCADA stream is calculated. Because these residual distributions are non-stationary and fluctuate with weather events and crop growth stages, static thresholds remain inadequate. To resolve this, the Peaks Over Threshold (POT) method, grounded in Extreme Value Theory (EVT), is applied to the sequence of residuals. POT dynamically establishes an adaptive anomaly threshold based on the statistical tail distribution of the errors. If the prediction residual of a new incoming data point exceeds this dynamically calculated POT limit, it is instantly classified as an anomaly, allowing the system to accurately flag true faults without being triggered by unexpected but natural environmental shifts7.

### **Physics-Informed Validation and Spatial Coherence**

Deep learning models, while mathematically powerful, operate fundamentally as "black boxes" that do not inherently understand the physical laws governing a greenhouse environment15. This lack of physical grounding can lead to severe false positive rates. For example, a purely data-driven AI might flag a sudden, massive drop in humidity as a spike fault, failing to recognize that the SCADA system had just commanded a ventilation screen to open fully. To prevent these false alarms, anomaly detection algorithms must be grounded in physical reality and operational context15.  
Psychrometric and thermodynamic constraints act as a foundational validation layer. Because temperature and relative humidity share an inverse thermodynamic relationship, a SCADA anomaly system can leverage mass balance and psychrometric laws8. If a humidity sensor suddenly reports a massive spike while the local temperature sensor simultaneously reports a rapid increase, the data violates fundamental physical constraints. This divergence immediately indicates a sensor fault or hardware malfunction, completely bypassing the need for complex neural network inference8. The integration of these rules—often termed Physics-Informed Large Language Models (PILLM) or hybrid rule-based agents—ensures that the anomaly detection system's outputs remain adaptive yet strictly bounded by physical plausibility15.  
Furthermore, in large commercial greenhouses, multiple sensors of the same type are distributed across different physical zones. While strict triple modular redundancy is cost-prohibitive for vast agricultural deployments, the spatial coherence between these sensors can be leveraged mathematically using an Autoregressive model paired with a Nested Kalman Filter1. The Nested Kalman Filter utilizes the data from physically proximate neighbor sensors to continuously evaluate the health of a target sensor, entirely negating the need for labeled training data10.

The Outer Filter acts as the refinement model. It receives the baseline prediction from the inner filter and refines it against the actual, real-time measurement of the target sensor under evaluation18. Based on the statistical deviation between the expected neighbor-derived value and the actual target value, the system classifies the target sensor's status into one of three operational states18. If the reading diverges beyond four standard deviations, it is classified as severely defective. The outer filter discards the target sensor's reading entirely and substitutes it with a synthetic value generated via bicubic interpolation from the neighboring sensors18. This nested approach ensures high data integrity and provides SCADA operators with continuous, reliable telemetry even during catastrophic localized sensor failures.

## **The Priva Climate Control Ecosystem and Hardware Topologies**

The efficacy of the aforementioned mathematical models and algorithmic frameworks is entirely predicated upon the reliable, high-frequency extraction of telemetry from the underlying control hardware. In the context of advanced protected horticulture, the Priva ecosystem represents one of the foremost global standards for automation and SCADA integration19. Priva engineers a tiered architecture of process computers, each scaling in complexity and capacity, designed to aggregate localized sensor data and govern complex greenhouse actuators, including roof vents, boiler networks, irrigation valves, and modulating LED lighting arrays19.

| Process Computer Model | Architectural Scope | Key Integrated Control Capabilities |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Priva Connext** | Up to 250 Compartments | The flagship system for large-scale, high-tech facilities. Executes advanced algorithms including Ratio Temperature to Radiation (RTR) tracking, photosynthesis optimization, modulating LED control, integrated water disinfection (Vialux), and comprehensive energy flow management19. |
| **Priva Compact CC** | Up to 27 Compartments | Designed for mid-tier modern greenhouses. Features a modular architecture handling essential controls such as complex ventilation, heat management, CO₂ dosing, Ebb/Flood systems, and the proprietary Moisture Balance Module21. |
| **Priva Compass** | Up to 20 Compartments | A streamlined system for smaller or less complex operations, providing core climate, water, and energy control with a highly intuitive user interface19. |

These process computers act as the central nervous system of the greenhouse, serving as the direct aggregators of physical sensor telemetry. They interface with hundreds of disparate end devices and third-party systems. This includes basic environmental probes, such as NTC thermistors, solid-state wind direction detectors, and optical precipitation intensity sensors, as well as highly specialized agricultural technology25. Priva's open platform design allows for deep integration with advanced wireless sensor fleets, such as Aranet systems measuring photosynthetically active radiation, soil moisture, and stem diameter6, and Wireless Value sensors providing direct inputs for crop weight and substrate conditions20. Furthermore, the system integrates data from third-party analytical platforms, such as Gardin's real-time plant health and photosynthetic efficiency indices26, and Grodan's GroSens root zone intelligence27.  
For a SCADA engineer, the primary concern is understanding the scope of accessible data within this vast ecosystem. According to Priva's architectural specifications, the specific datapoints available for extraction are strictly dependent on three variables: the physical sensors connected to the installation, the software control modules licensed and active within the specific Connext, Compact CC, or Compass computer, and the specific firmware version running on the hardware28. As a general integration heuristic, any datapoint, measurement, alarm state, or calculated value that is available to be visualized in the charts of the Priva Operator—the primary web application and user interface for climate management—is actively historized and available for API retrieval24. Standardized mappings of these datapoints are maintained by Priva in localized reference packages to assist developers in mapping the facility's data topology28.

## **Extracting SCADA Telemetry via Priva Cloud API Infrastructure**

Extracting data from Priva systems for external SCADA anomaly detection requires navigating either their modern cloud-based API infrastructure or traditional on-premise industrial protocols. For modern deployments and large-scale integrations, Priva strongly mandates the use of their centralized cloud environment, built upon the Microsoft Azure platform24. The local process computer interfaces securely with the cloud via a physical edge device known as the Priva Edge Gateway, ensuring a secure, outbound-only telemetry flow that complies with ISO 27001 and ISO 9001 security standards30.  
To interface a third-party SCADA system, AI backend, or custom anomaly detection pipeline with this data, engineers must utilize the Priva Horticulture API Services, accessible via the dedicated developer portal32. Accessing this portal and the underlying endpoints requires the facility to hold an active Priva Connected subscription26.  
The Priva API architecture is heavily secured using OpenID Connect and OAuth 2.0 compliant Identity and Access Management (IAM) services, meaning integration cannot rely on standard user credentials or basic authentication32. The integration process requires a programmatic Service Principal. The greenhouse administrator must log into the Priva Access Control App and register the SCADA system as a trusted external application, which generates a unique Client ID and Client Secret32. The SCADA backend must then initiate a standard HTTP POST request to the authentication endpoint formatted as an application/x-www-form-urlencoded payload containing the grant type, client credentials, and the required scope parameter32. Upon validation, the IAM service returns a JSON Web Token (JWT), which must be included as a Bearer token in the Authorization header of all subsequent HTTP API requests32.  
Once authenticated, the SCADA backend interacts with several distinct API endpoints to aggregate the necessary telemetry for anomaly detection modeling.

| API Endpoint Service | Integration Functionality | SCADA Anomaly Detection Application |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Data Insight Metadata API** | Provides access to variable metadata saved to the cloud, detailing the structural hierarchy of the facility. | Essential for initial topology mapping. Allows the SCADA system to automatically discover connected sensors and match abstract system IDs to physical greenhouse compartments32. |
| **Data Insight History API** | Grants access to historical variable values and long-term storage data via defined time-range queries. | Critical for extracting the massive, multi-month datasets required to train Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, Autoencoders, and establish EWMA baselines32. |
| **Realtime Telemetry API** | Manages publish/subscribe architectures, event hub creation, and datapoint subscriptions using Change of Value (COV) reporting. | Serves as the primary pipeline for live anomaly inference. COV reporting ensures the SCADA system receives updates only when physical measurements change, drastically reducing network overhead and latency32. |
| **Realtime Setpoint API** | Permits the programmatic alteration of datapoints, control strategies, and mechanical setpoints across the internet. | Enables autonomous remediation. If the anomaly detection algorithm identifies a critical sensor failure, this API can automatically shift the Priva control strategy to a safe fallback mode or inject corrected Kalman filter estimates32. |
| **Notification API** | Enables external systems to receive real-time alarm notifications from Priva systems via webhooks. | Allows the SCADA system to correlate algorithmic anomalies with native Priva hardware alarms, providing context for complex failure states32. |

All API payloads are structured in standard JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) format35. This standardization is highly beneficial for data science workflows, as the nested JSON responses can be seamlessly deserialized into Python-based analytical environments, such as Pandas DataFrames, for immediate algorithmic evaluation, min-max normalization, and residual calculation.

## **Localized and Legacy On-Premise Integration Pathways**

While the Azure-backed cloud APIs provide robust scalability and ease of integration for modern architectures, certain highly secure, remote, or legacy agricultural facilities demand strictly localized, air-gapped SCADA integration. Priva's on-premise infrastructure supports several methods for direct data extraction without reliance on external internet connectivity.  
Prior to the industry-wide transition to cloud-based telemetry, Priva systems utilized the Top Control 8 software suite, managed locally via the Priva Office Direct or TC Manager applications23. In these environments, historical telemetry is aggregated and managed by a dedicated application known as TC History41. TC History typically resides on a local Windows Server operating system or directly on a specialized, DIN-rail mounted industrial PC known as the Priva Blue ID SX10041.  
TC History natively stores all operational tables, graphs, alarm messages, and continuous process data within a Microsoft SQL Server database environment, frequently utilizing MS SQL Server 2014, 2016, or SQL Server Express41. In highly constrained deployments, MS Access databases may be utilized, though these are strictly limited to a two-gigabyte capacity and are entirely incompatible with the SX100 hardware platform41. SCADA systems can interface directly with these localized SQL databases using standard Open Database Connectivity (ODBC) or Java Database Connectivity (JDBC) drivers. By executing read-only SQL queries, the SCADA backend can extract raw, high-frequency sensor logs directly from the historian tables. Alternatively, data can be retrieved programmatically via the local TC LAN Manager or TC History Proxy services42.  
For direct, real-time communication between the Priva process computer and a local SCADA Programmable Logic Controller (PLC), standardized industrial protocols are necessary. Priva's Blue ID and Comforte controllers are engineered as native BACnet Application Specific Controllers (B-ASC). They communicate openly via certified BACnet protocols over TCP/IP, allowing any compliant SCADA system to discover and read sensor objects directly from the local network without the need for proprietary software translation layers39.  
Furthermore, for localized sensor networks and third-party hardware integrations, Priva systems frequently utilize Modbus RTU and TCP protocols. The proprietary Priva Roombus interface is, at its core, an implementation of the standard Modbus protocol40. In advanced industrial setups, OPC Unified Architecture (OPC UA) represents the modern standard for secure, cross-platform communication. While traditional OPC server and client architectures have long been used to bridge PC applications to Priva hardware43, modern OPC UA integration is often facilitated through intermediate gateway platforms or third-party PLCs (such as Beckhoff TwinCAT systems) acting as protocol translators to securely route telemetry from the Priva plant area into the broader corporate SCADA network46.

## **Synthesis: Operationalizing the SCADA Anomaly Pipeline**

The successful integration of these mathematical anomaly detection models with the Priva data streams involves a highly structured, multi-stage data engineering pipeline.  
Initially, the SCADA system establishes a continuous telemetry stream, utilizing either the JSON-based Priva Realtime Telemetry API for cloud environments or local OPC UA and BACnet subscriptions for on-premise deployments32. The incoming data is parsed, and the queried metadata is utilized to map abstract variables to precise physical greenhouse compartments32. This raw data is then passed through a preprocessing layer, where min-max normalization standardizes all measurements, converting disparate values—such as a 35,000 lux light reading and a 24°C temperature reading—onto a uniform computational scale7.  
Crucially, the normalized data is then evaluated against the physics-informed contextual validation engine. The telemetry is checked against psychrometric rules and mass-balance constraints8. If a sudden drop in ambient humidity correlates directly with an actuator command opening a roof vent—data also extracted simultaneously from the Priva telemetry stream—the physics-informed logic suppresses the anomaly detection algorithm, acknowledging the event as a legitimate mechanical state change rather than a sensor fault15.  
Only the cleaned, physically validated telemetry array is fed into the deep learning architectures, such as the CNN-LSTM-POT model, or the statistical Nested Kalman Filter state-space7. These models run continuous inference, calculating prediction residuals and updating their dynamic extreme-value thresholds in real-time. If the residual deviation of a sensor exceeds the dynamically calculated POT threshold, or breaches the severe four-standard-deviation limit of the outer Kalman filter, a definitive anomaly is declared7.  
Upon detection, the SCADA system records the event, categorizes the fault profile (distinguishing between calibration drift, bias, or a spike fault), and triggers a high-priority alert for maintenance personnel. In fully autonomous deployments leveraging the Priva Realtime Setpoint API, the SCADA system can actively suppress the erroneous data from reaching the primary control loops, substituting it with interpolated neighbor-sensor data to maintain the stable operation of the Priva Connext climate strategies until physical repairs are completed18. By combining precise physical fault etiology, advanced neural and statistical mathematics, and deep integration with the Priva control ecosystem, SCADA engineers can ensure the absolute cyber-physical integrity of the modern agricultural enterprise.
